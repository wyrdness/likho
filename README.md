## Overview

Likho (Russian and Ukrainian Лихо, Belarusian лі́ха, Polish licho) is the Slavic embodiment of evil fate and misfortune. It appears in East Slavic and West Slavic fairy tales and proverbs as a one-eyed creature — typically an emaciated old woman dressed in black, occasionally an evil male goblin of the forest — whose touch or company brings ill luck. The word itself is a common noun meaning 'bad luck' or 'evil' in modern Russian and Ukrainian, and its root in Old Russian carried the sense of 'excessive' or 'odd', from which the pejorative use developed. Unlike major Slavic mythological figures such as Perun or Veles, Likho is not part of any canonical pantheon; instead it survives in folklore through cautionary tales and idioms (the Russian and Ukrainian proverb 'Don't wake Likho while it is quiet'). In one well-known fairy tale a blacksmith encounters Likho in a hut deep in the forest; she demands he forge her a new eye, but the smith blinds her remaining eye instead and escapes. Other narratives warn that Likho clings to its victims and cannot be drowned, and that one can only be rid of her by passing the curse to another.

## Etymology

- **Origin**: Common Slavic noun meaning 'evil' or 'misfortune'. In Old Russian likho meant 'excessive', 'too much', 'remaining' or 'odd' (as opposed to even); the pejorative sense developed from the idea of an unlucky odd number. Most likely derived from Indo-European *leikʷ- 'to remain, leave behind'.
- **Meaning**: 'Evil', 'bad luck', 'misfortune'; as a personification, the spirit that brings these.
- **First use**: Attested in Slavic folktale traditions; widely recorded in 19th-century Russian, Ukrainian, Belarusian, and Polish folklore collections.

## Theories & Explanations

### Skeptical

**Personification of abstract misfortune** _(Mike Dixon-Kennedy)_

Likho is best understood as a folk-literary personification of bad luck, not a claimed real entity. The figure functions to externalise the experience of misfortune in narrative form.

**Cyclops tale-type parallel** _(Comparative folklorists)_

The most-cited Likho tale (the smith blinding Likho's eye and escaping under a sheep) is structurally a Slavic version of the international Polyphemus tale type (ATU 1137).
